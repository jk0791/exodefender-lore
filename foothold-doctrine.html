The Foothold Doctrine

An account of humanity's strategic response to the C'tarr-ni threat, compiled from the archives of the Interstellar Settlement Initiative.

The Enemy

Following the destruction of humanity's first extraterrestrial colonies, an extensive programme of robotic reconnaissance was undertaken to learn more about the C'tarr-ni.

The findings were deeply troubling.

Most habitable worlds within a hundred light-years of Earth showed evidence of C'tarr-ni activity, ranging from scattered artefacts to extensive structures.

Analysis of these discoveries gradually revealed a civilisation governed by deeply held religious beliefs. The C'tarr-ni considered themselves divinely appointed rulers of the galaxy, and believed that the right to travel between the stars belonged exclusively to them.

Humanity's expansion was not merely unwelcome. To the C'tarr-ni, it was an act of heresy.

Yet the reconnaissance programme also revealed a potentially decisive weakness.

Despite their technological superiority, the C'tarr-ni appeared to be constrained by the same fundamental limitations of quantum assembly and quantum transmission that governed humanity's own interstellar expansion.

They could no more deploy vast armies, fleets or stockpiles of weapons to distant worlds than humanity could.

Their greatest advantage was not an ability to move freely across the galaxy, but the enormous head start they had enjoyed in exploring and occupying it.

Further analysis suggested that, despite their long history of interstellar expansion, the C'tarr-ni had not ventured beyond the Local Bubble of the Orion Arm.

A New Strategy

Earth's reconnaissance programme began reaching progressively farther into the galaxy.

Eventually, thousands of light-years from Earth, probes identified a region of the Orion Arm containing numerous potentially habitable planets and moons.

More importantly, the region appeared to be untouched by the C'tarr-ni.

A shortlist of promising worlds was prepared.

But this time, humanity would not send colonists.

It would send soldiers.

The strategy calls for the deployment of military engineers and air support to establish and progressively strengthen bases on multiple distant worlds.

Their remoteness offers two critical advantages.

First, the installations may be developed without attracting the attention of the C'tarr-ni.

Second, even if discovered, the enormous distances involved and the inherent limitations of quantum transmission prevent the enemy from deploying substantial forces quickly.

For the first time, humanity has an opportunity to meet a C'tarr-ni attack with comparable, or even overwhelming, military strength.

The ultimate objective is not simply to establish isolated military outposts.

It is to create a growing network of strongholds, eventually reaching a critical mass of defensive capability sufficient to secure an entire region of space.

Within that protected region, humanity may finally be able to establish permanent civilian colonies.

This time, they will not be defenceless.

Whether the strategy succeeds will depend on the survival of the individual footholds.
