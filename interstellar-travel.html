Interstellar Travel

The Breakthrough

Humanity's ability to travel between the stars emerged from two extraordinary scientific discoveries, separated by nearly eighty years.

Quantum Transmission

Quantum transmission was first demonstrated approximately a century before the establishment of New Dawn.

Early experiments succeeded in transferring minute quantities of matter between two precisely calibrated devices: a transmitter and a receiver.

The transmitter was an enormously complex installation requiring substantial energy, while the receiver was comparatively small and simple.

For decades, the technology remained confined to laboratory experiments. The quantities of matter that could be transmitted were microscopic, and attempts to increase the scale of transmission encountered formidable technical difficulties.

Progress was slow.

Nearly a century of research and development was required before engineers succeeded in constructing a transmission system with a receiver aperture measuring approximately sixty centimetres.

By then, the energy requirements had grown so enormous that practical transmission installations could only be constructed and operated in space, using the resources of large orbital facilities.

Despite these achievements, quantum transmission remained fundamentally limited.

Matter could only be transmitted to an existing receiver.

And there was no known way to transport a receiver to another star system.

Remote Quantum Assembly

Approximately twenty years before the establishment of New Dawn, a second breakthrough transformed the prospects for interstellar travel.

Quantum assembly was already a well-established manufacturing technology, allowing engineers to synthesise elements and molecules, grow complex components and assemble them into functioning machines.

The breakthrough came with the discovery of a method for remotely influencing quantum particles, allowing these established manufacturing processes to be controlled across enormous distances.

For the first time, engineers could construct physical objects at distant locations using matter already present at the destination, without transporting the machinery required to build them.

The process was extraordinarily slow and required the near-weightless conditions and vacuum of space.

Nevertheless, its implications were profound.

The most important application was immediately apparent.

Remote quantum assembly could be used to construct a quantum transmission receiver in orbit around a distant world.

Once completed, that receiver could accept matter transmitted from Earth.

The two technologies, developed independently over the course of a century, had finally made interstellar travel possible.

A War of Logistics

Despite the extraordinary advances in quantum transmission and remote quantum assembly, interstellar travel remains an enormously complex and resource-intensive undertaking.

In the war between humanity and the C’tarr-ni, both sides face the same fundamental challenge: projecting military power across the vast distances between the stars.

Establishing a Receiver

The first challenge is constructing a quantum transmission receiver at the destination.

Remote quantum assembly is an extraordinarily slow process, requiring the precise manipulation of matter over interstellar distances. The receiver must be constructed in orbit, where the near-weightless conditions and vacuum necessary for quantum assembly can be maintained.

Even after a suitable planet or moon has been identified, establishing a functioning receiver requires considerable time.

Only then can the first physical objects be transmitted from Earth.

Transmitting Personnel and Equipment

The second challenge is the severe limitation on transmission capacity.

Quantum transmission receivers have an aperture of approximately sixty centimetres, and the transmission of even a relatively small object takes several hours.

Larger objects must be divided into components, transmitted separately and reassembled at their destination.

Every person, every piece of equipment and every component of a vehicle or structure must pass through this narrow bottleneck.

Although Earth possesses the industrial capacity to manufacture enormous quantities of equipment, its ability to deliver those resources to distant worlds is severely constrained.

Reaching the Surface

Arriving at a distant world is only part of the journey.

Quantum transmission must take place in orbit, under near-weightless conditions and in a vacuum. Personnel and equipment therefore arrive aboard an orbital receiving installation, not on the planet's surface.

Transporting them to the surface requires additional spacecraft, equipment and supporting infrastructure.

Much of this infrastructure must itself be transmitted in components and assembled at the destination.

Consequently, establishing even a modest surface installation requires a substantial investment of time and resources before meaningful operations can begin.

The One-Way Journey

Perhaps the most profound limitation of quantum transmission is that interstellar travel is, for practical purposes, one-way.

While receivers are relatively small and simple, transmitters are enormous industrial installations requiring extraordinary amounts of energy.

Constructing a transmitter on a distant world would require an industrial and energy infrastructure far beyond the capabilities of an emerging colony or military outpost.

Although limited communication can be maintained between star systems, personnel and equipment cannot simply be transmitted back to Earth.

For those who make the journey, departure is generally a permanent commitment.

The Consequences

These limitations fundamentally shape the conduct of interstellar warfare.

Establishing and supplying even a small base requires years of planning, construction and sustained logistical support.

The deployment of large military formations, fleets of spacecraft or substantial stockpiles of weapons is impractical. Reinforcements must be transmitted incrementally, and the process cannot be accelerated simply by committing additional resources.

The C'tarr-ni, despite their technological superiority, are constrained by the same fundamental limitations.

As a result, interstellar warfare is conducted by relatively small forces operating at enormous distances from their homeworlds.

Military installations must be capable of defending themselves with the personnel, equipment and weapons already available to them.

When an installation comes under attack, additional forces may be dispatched, but they cannot be expected to arrive in time to influence an immediate engagement.

The survival of each foothold depends upon the capabilities of the forces already deployed.

Humanity has acquired the means to cross the vast distances between the stars.

Overcoming the logistical challenges of establishing and defending a permanent presence among them remains one of its greatest challenges.
